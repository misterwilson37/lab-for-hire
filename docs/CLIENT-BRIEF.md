# Client brief: Hanson's words

Hanson is the 7th-grade science teacher this is built for. Jake Wilson (Computer Science related-arts teacher, same school) builds it. These are her two messages **verbatim**. When a design question comes up, check what she actually asked for.

## Message 1: the original request

> What a dangerous statement to make! 😂 Ok, enter into my "Hanson's Barbie Dreamhouse" of science game play...
>
> Our biggest gap in science is getting students to read, understand, and apply the concepts we teach using data tables, charts, and graphs. They need repeated practice, and they've specifically told us they want something game-like that they would actually choose to use.
>
> What appeals to me about Deck.Toys is the adventure aspect, the ability to following paths, complete challenges, and unlock things. I'd love to combine that with the replayability students enjoy in Blooket and the branching support of a Google Form.
>
> The gameplay is a huge priority because I want students to enjoy the actual experience enough to keep coming back, with reading and interpreting data built into how they succeed. Choices, missions, collecting items, upgrading something, or solving mysteries could all be possibilities. I'm very open to your ideas about what would make it genuinely fun!
>
> Here's what I'd love the learning side to include:
>
> 1. Several questions connected to one graph or table, with the visual remaining visible and easy to read.
> 2. A range of thinking, from reading values and identifying trends to comparing results, evaluating claims, selecting evidence, and applying science concepts.
> 3. Multiple-choice, pick two, and select-all-that-apply questions.
> 4. Support that branches based on their answers. For example, if a student misreads the scale, they receive a quick explanation and guided practice with the scale. If they read the graph correctly but misunderstand the science, they receive reteaching on that concept instead.
> 5. A chance to apply that help to a new question before returning to the main challenge. Ideally, getting help would feel like part of the game and keep them moving.
> 6. Repeated practice with different data. Students would encounter the same skills across new graphs, tables, and scenarios so they build understanding instead of memorizing answers.
>
> For example, a mission could involve choosing a material for a cold pack. Students would examine temperature data, compare materials, and use evidence to make a choice. An incorrect answer could trigger a short, targeted side mission, followed by a fresh challenge to see whether the support helped.
>
> The balance I'm hoping for is enough repetition to build confidence and skill, with enough variety and meaningful gameplay that students want another round. Students also need time to think, speed shouldn't be the main way to win. I'd also love for them to access it independently during class or at home through a link in Google Classroom.
>
> I have no idea how much of this is possible, but I appreciate your willingness to partner with us!

## Message 2: feedback after seeing the demo

> Ok, after taking some more time to look into this, please don't abandon it! 😂 I think you've given us a really incredibly starting point and I'm amazed.
>
> Your question about whether it's fun has me thinking: what if students could actually build and run their own lab? They could accept client jobs, earn money from completing investigations, and use it to purchase equipment, customize their space, and unlock new types of cases. Having something that belongs to them and continues to grow might give them a reason to come back.
>
> There's also a real instructional opportunity here. Our updated standards ask students to plan investigations, analyze evidence, develop models, and apply their understanding. Those are areas where they need more practice, and running a lab could give those skills a purpose.
>
> For example, a client could need help choosing a material for a particular purpose. Students would decide how to compare the options fairly, examine the resulting data, answer several connected questions, and make a recommendation supported by evidence. Completing the job would earn something they could use in their lab. A later client could have different requirements or new data, so students would need to work through the evidence again.
>
> Maybe we could eventually include medical design cases tied to 7.ETS1.1, reaction investigations tied to 7.PS3.1, or plant-growth investigations tied to 7.LS1.4? That would let us keep the same game going as our content changes throughout the year.
>
> I'd absolutely want to keep the branching support you've already built, it is awesome! My thought is that giving students more ownership and meaningful choices might create the "I want to play another round" feeling we're looking for. Could be totally wrong.
>
> I have no idea if any of this is possible, but I can help with the content, standards alignment, common misconceptions, and student feedback if that helps at all! Thank you again for taking this idea and making it something we can actually explore! Let me know if an in person time to chat and look at options would be helpful. THANK YOU!!!!

## Context from Jake
- Lesson that started the project (Particle Motion Lesson 5, standard 7.PS1.3): students take data from a table, make a line graph and then a phase diagram, and answer test-style questions about reading them. The lesson-plan synopsis: "Students are expected to be able to read charts, graphs, phase diagrams, etc in order to extrapolate information and answer questions about the information in the graph." The game is **spiral review of those skills**, since the upcoming scope leaves little room to practice them.
- Her lesson's anticipated misconceptions became error tags: picking the highest-pressure point for greatest kinetic energy (`SCI-KE-TEMP`); reading the phase right but matching the wrong particle motion (`SCI-MOTION-STATES`); assuming all graphs communicate the same information (`SCI-GRAPH-PURPOSE`).
- The unit test (Unit 9, Particle Motion and Arrangement) shaped further tags: particles themselves change (`SCI-PARTICLES-CHANGE`), wrong source of pressure (`SCI-PRESSURE-SOURCE`), compressibility (`SCI-COMPRESS`), non-particle explanations (`SCI-PARTICLE-MODEL`), and a log-scale pressure axis. **No test or worksheet item is used.**
- The unit test's Table 2 has physically inconsistent numbers (a 120 kPa "vacuum" is above atmospheric pressure). Worth mentioning to Hanson; not reused.
